The Psychology of UI

An interactive lab where you don't just read about cognitive principles — you experience them on your own brain.

How to use: Navigate via the sidebar. Each demo proves a principle viscerally — that moment is when it becomes intuition.

Core thesis

A UI is not a visual artifact. It's a cognitive prosthetic. Every pixel either reduces or increases mental work.

The user's brain is a fast, lazy, biased pattern-matcher with a 4-slot working memory operating on 200ms reflexes. Your interface either cooperates with this brain or fights it.

Pre-attentive Vision

Before conscious thought engages (~200ms), your visual cortex has already processed color, orientation, size, motion. This is why some elements "pop" without effort.

Why: Color is the strongest pre-attentive feature. Your primary CTA must differ from secondary buttons by color, not just label.

Gestalt Principles

The brain perceives wholes before parts. Identical elements group differently based on proximity and similarity.

Proximity

Move the slider. The brain perceives "rows" or "columns" purely from distance.

Similarity

Toggle similarity. With identical colors → 4×4 grid. With color groups → columns. Same spacing.

Takeaway: Form labels must hug their inputs (proximity). Same-colored buttons feel like they do the same thing (similarity).

T = a + b·log₂(n). Decision time grows with options.

The way we read

Reading isn't linear scanning of letters. The eye performs saccades — rapid jumps — pausing on fixations every 200–250ms, recognizing word shapes more than individual characters. When line length exceeds about 75 characters, the eye loses its place returning to the next line.

Try Comic Sans. The same content suddenly feels less credible — the Baskerville Effect in reverse. Typography signals authority before content is read.

Why developers love TypeScript: a deep dive

TypeScript has become the de facto language for building large-scale JavaScript applications. Its type system catches bugs at compile time that would otherwise surface at runtime, dramatically reducing production incidents.

Beyond bug-prevention, TypeScript serves as living documentation. Types describe intent in a way comments cannot, because they are checked by the compiler.

However, adoption requires investment. Teams must learn structural typing, generics, and the nuances of inference. The payoff comes after the learning curve flattens.

Front-load important words. Left-aligned bullets get seen; right-aligned ones don't.

Feedback Latency

The Halo Effect

A single positive trait colors perception of unrelated traits. Beautiful UI is rated more functional and trustworthy.

For every design decision, ask "why?" five times. If you can't answer, delete it.
