The Psychology of UI

An interactive lab built on a single thesis: a UI is not a visual artifact, it's a cognitive prosthetic. Every pixel either reduces or increases the mental work a user does to translate intent into action.

How each chapter is structured

You'll see four layers per topic:

  1. Theory — what's happening in the brain and why this principle exists.
  2. Demo — interactive experience so you feel the principle.
  3. What to do — concrete rules you apply to your code.
  4. Anti-patterns — what fails and why.

The mental model to adopt

Imagine the user as a fast, lazy, biased pattern-matcher with a 4-slot working memory operating on 200ms reflexes. They don't read — they recognize. They don't analyze — they react. Your job is to cooperate with that brain.

Chapter 1 · Perception

Pre-attentive Vision

Your visual cortex processes color, motion, orientation, and size before conscious attention engages — within ~200ms. Designing with this in mind is the difference between a UI that feels effortless and one that feels exhausting.

The Theory

Vision happens in stages: (1) pre-attentive (~50ms, automatic feature detection), (2) attentive (~200–500ms, focused selection), (3) cognitive interpretation (500ms+, meaning assignment, requires working memory).

Pre-attentive features include color hue, intensity, size, orientation, motion, and curvature. The brain detects these in parallel across the entire visual field — no scanning required.

Conjunction search (e.g., "find the red square among red circles and blue squares") fails the pre-attentive system — it requires serial scanning. This is why icons that differ only by combination of features are slow to find.

✓ What to do
  • Differentiate primary CTAs by color, not just label. A blue "Save" among gray "Cancel/Edit/Delete" buttons is found in <200ms. The same buttons all gray with different labels take 1–2s to scan.
  • Use saturation as a hierarchy lever. Saturated colors say "look here." Reserve them for ~5% of the screen. The rest should be muted (Stripe, Linear, Vercel all follow this).
  • For status indication (online dot, unread badge), rely on color first. A red dot on a notification icon is processed pre-attentively; a number "3" requires reading.
  • Use motion sparingly but powerfully. Motion is the strongest pre-attentive feature — it commands attention against the user's will. Reserve it for genuinely critical events (errors, new messages), never decorative loops.
  • Test with the "5-second find" rule: Show your screen for 5 seconds. Can a stranger identify the primary action? If not, your pre-attentive design has failed.
✗ Anti-patterns
  • Mystery icon toolbars (16 monochrome icons in a row). Conjunction search fails — users hover one-by-one to find what they need.
  • Three "primary" buttons of equal weight. When everything pops, nothing pops. The eye has no salience signal and defaults to scanning left-to-right.
  • Decorative animations on landing pages (particles, looping gradients). They consume the user's involuntary attention budget so your CTA gets less.
  • Brand red = error red. Now error states blend into branding. Functional pre-attentive signals get drowned out.
Chapter 2 · Perception

Gestalt Principles

"The whole is greater than the sum of its parts." Identical elements group into different meanings based purely on their spatial relationships.

The Theory

Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka, ~1920s) discovered that the visual system performs automatic grouping as part of perception itself. You don't see "8 dots" — you see "two rows of 4" or "four columns of 2", depending on spacing. The grouping is involuntary.

Key laws relevant to UI:

  • Proximity: close = related
  • Similarity: same color/shape/size = same function
  • Common region: shared boundary = grouped
  • Closure: brain completes incomplete shapes
  • Continuity: eye follows lines and curves
  • Figure/ground: separates foreground from background

These aren't aesthetic preferences. They're how the visual cortex literally constructs the scene before you become conscious of it.

✓ What to do
  • Form labels and inputs: place labels within ~8px of their input. Floating labels at equal distance from two fields cause errors. Rule: distance from label to its input must be smaller than distance to anything else.
  • Spacing between groups: use ~2× the inner spacing. If items inside a group are 8px apart, groups should be 16–24px apart. The 2× ratio is what the brain reads as "different group."
  • Card-based layouts exploit common region. When unrelated information must coexist on a screen, wrap each cluster in a card to prevent cross-group confusion.
  • Reuse style for same function. All destructive actions: same red. All primary actions: same blue. Similarity is a contract — break it and trust collapses.
  • Use alignment as continuity. Aligning elements creates invisible "rails" the eye follows. This is why grids work — they multiply continuity cues across the whole screen.
✗ Anti-patterns
  • Equidistant labels: a label floating equally between two fields belongs to neither — users guess and often pick wrong.
  • Inconsistent button colors for same action. If "Save" is blue on one screen and green on another, users believe they do different things.
  • Borders everywhere. Over-bordering fights closure — the brain doesn't need lines if proximity already groups things. Whitespace is cheaper and cleaner.
  • Misaligned columns by 1–3px. Worse than obviously broken — the brain notices the inconsistency subliminally and the page "feels off."
Chapter 3 · Cognition

Cognitive Load Theory

Working memory holds ~4 chunks. Every unexplained icon, jargon term, or context switch spends from this finite budget. Bad UI is bad accounting.

The Theory

John Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory (1988) splits mental effort into three types:

  • Intrinsic load: inherent task difficulty (filing taxes is hard, period).
  • Extraneous load: load from poor presentation — the load you create.
  • Germane load: load from learning/schema-building (productive).

Cowan (2001) revised Miller's famous "7±2" downward to ~4 chunks for true working memory. Anything beyond that is offloaded to recognition (seeing it on screen) or external memory (notes, scratchpads).

Your job: ruthlessly minimize extraneous load, leaving the user's working memory free for their actual goal.

Demo: Working memory test

A 7-digit sequence appears for 2 seconds. Try to recall it.

✓ What to do
  • Recognition over recall. If a user must remember something between screens, surface it (breadcrumbs, summary panels, "review your order"). Show, don't ask them to remember.
  • Chunk long forms. 30 fields on one page = catastrophic. 30 fields in 5 sections of 6 = manageable. Sections create cognitive "bookmarks."
  • Progressive disclosure. Show the 80% case by default; hide advanced options behind "Show more." This is why settings panels have "Advanced" sections.
  • Auto-fill, auto-detect, auto-format. Don't make users type their address when their browser knows it. Don't make them format a phone number. Every avoided keystroke is working-memory recovered.
  • One primary action per screen. Multiple primaries fragment attention and force System 2 evaluation ("which should I click?").
  • Use familiar patterns. A hamburger means menu. A magnifying glass means search. Reusing schemas means the user spends zero load learning your UI.
✗ Anti-patterns
  • Wizard with no progress indicator. User must remember "what step am I on, what's left." Add Step 3 of 5.
  • Modal-on-modal. Stacks context the user must hold. By the time they're 3 modals deep, they don't remember why they started.
  • Cryptic error codes: Error 0x80070005. Forces user to research; pure extraneous load. Replace with: "You don't have permission. Ask your admin or try logging in again."
  • Verification codes that don't autofill. Forces user to memorize 6 digits across two apps. Use OTP autofill APIs.
  • Re-asking known information. If you know their email from signup, never ask again. Persistence reduces load.
Chapter 4 · Attention

Visual Hierarchy

Hierarchy is the deliberate manipulation of pre-attentive features so the brain processes elements in the order you intend. Without hierarchy, the user's eye wanders.

The Theory

The eye is drawn to elements with greater visual weight — a function of size, contrast, color saturation, isolation (whitespace around it), and position. The element with the most weight is processed first; subsequent elements form a descending sequence.

Hierarchy works because the brain is metabolic-cost-averse: it processes the highest-signal element first to extract maximum information per joule. If everything has equal weight, the brain has no thermodynamic shortcut and falls back to slow scanning.

Three levels of hierarchy is the sweet spot: dominant, secondary, tertiary. More than three flattens into noise.

✓ What to do
  • Define a hierarchy contract: on every screen, decide which element is dominant (the "hero"), which 1–2 are secondary, and which are tertiary. Everything else is body.
  • Use a typographic scale. Pick a ratio (1.25 or 1.333) and stick to it: 12, 15, 18, 22, 28, 35. The brain reads proportional differences pre-attentively.
  • Use weight + size together. A 24px bold heading beats a 32px regular one for hierarchy. Weight is cheaper than size and crowds the layout less.
  • Whitespace amplifies importance. Surround your primary CTA with empty space. Isolation = importance.
  • One color "loud" per screen. If your CTA is saturated purple, everything else should be grayscale or muted. Saturation budget is small.

✗ Anti-patterns
  • Equal-weight CTAs: "Save" and "Cancel" both bold blue buttons. Add visual hierarchy — one solid, one outlined.
  • Decorative > functional: hero illustration the size of the screen, CTA tiny in the corner.
  • 5+ font sizes: hierarchy collapses into noise. Stick to 4–5 scale steps max.
  • Bold body text: if everything is bold, nothing is.
Chapter 5 · Attention

The Squint Test

A diagnostic tool. Squint at any UI until details blur. The shapes that remain are your real visual hierarchy. If your CTA disappears but a decoration pops, the hierarchy is inverted.

The Theory

Squinting strips away high-frequency detail (text, icons, fine borders) and leaves only low-frequency information (luminance blobs, large color regions). This simulates the user's peripheral vision and the first 50ms of perception.

If your hierarchy works at low resolution, it will work at full resolution too. The reverse isn't true: a UI that only "reads" at full focus has failed the speed test.

This is also called the 5-second test in usability research: show a screen for 5 seconds, ask the user what they remember. The blobs they describe are their squint-test perception.

✓ What to do
  • Run the squint test on every screen before shipping. Browsers can simulate this with filter: blur(6px) in DevTools.
  • The dominant element should remain visible when blurred — that's correct hierarchy.
  • If two elements pop equally when blurred, you have competing primaries. Demote one.
  • If your logo dominates the squint test, your branding is louder than your value proposition. Usually wrong on landing pages.
  • Apply it to dark mode and light mode separately. Hierarchy can collapse in one theme even if it works in the other.
✗ Anti-patterns
  • Hero image dominates, CTA disappears under blur. The image is winning the user's attention; the conversion goal is invisible.
  • Logo + nav + hero + CTA all visible at squint. Too many priorities; user has no anchor.
  • "Sign up" link in body text matches the visual weight of the actual CTA button. Demote it visually or remove it.
Chapter 6 · Interaction

Fitts's Law

Time to acquire a target = a + b · log₂(D/W + 1), where D is distance and W is target size. Bigger and closer = faster. This isn't a guideline — it's a measured law of motor control.

The Theory

Paul Fitts (1954) showed that pointing tasks have a logarithmic relationship between speed and the difficulty index log₂(D/W + 1). The law applies to mice, fingers, eye gaze, even foot pedals.

Practical consequences:

  • Doubling target size reduces acquisition time more than halving distance.
  • Screen edges have effectively infinite size — the cursor stops there. This is why macOS puts the menu bar at the top edge, and why "close" buttons often live in corners.
  • Mobile thumb zones: the bottom-center of phone screens is the easiest reach; top corners are the hardest.
  • Cursor proximity matters: a button near where the user's cursor already is gets clicked faster than one across the screen.

✓ What to do
  • Touch targets ≥ 44×44px (Apple HIG) or 48×48dp (Material). This isn't aesthetic — below this, error rates spike.
  • Place primary actions where the cursor/finger already is. "Save" buttons should follow the form; modal "Confirm" should be near the modal's content, not far away.
  • Use screen edges for global actions. Browser back, OS menu bar, mobile home indicator — these are infinite-edge real estate.
  • Mobile: put primary actions in the bottom thumb zone (bottom 25% of screen). Top-bar nav requires thumb gymnastics on large phones.
  • Increase hit area beyond visual size for icon buttons: a 16×16px icon can have a 44×44px clickable area via padding. Don't force users to aim precisely.

✗ Anti-patterns
  • Tiny "x" close buttons. A 12×12px close button has 4× the error rate of a 24×24px one.
  • "Confirm" placed far from the dialog content. Increases D in Fitts's equation, slows decisions.
  • Important nav links at the top of a long mobile screen. Forces thumb stretching that hurts retention.
  • Icon-only buttons with no padding around the icon. Visually small AND functionally small. Always pad the hit area.
Chapter 7 · Decision

Hick's Law

Decision time = a + b · log₂(n). More choices means slower decisions — and beyond a threshold, no decision at all (analysis paralysis).

The Theory

Hick (1952) measured that response time grows logarithmically with the number of equally likely choices. This is information-theoretic: the user is essentially performing binary search through their options.

The critical insight: the law assumes equal-weighted, flat choices. Two design strategies break the law in your favor:

  • Chunking: 20 options in 4 groups of 5 → cost is log₂(4) + log₂(5) ≈ 4.3 bits, vs. log₂(20) ≈ 4.3 bits flat. Same total bits but the user perceives it as easier because each step has fewer options.
  • Defaults / recommendations: a "Recommended" badge or pre-selected option collapses the decision to "accept default or override?" — effectively n=2.

Sheena Iyengar's Jam Study (2000) famously showed 24 jam varieties produced 10× fewer purchases than 6 varieties. More choice ≠ more conversion.

✓ What to do
  • Top-level nav: 5–7 items. Beyond 7, scan time exceeds patience. If you have more, group under categories.
  • Pricing tiers: 3, max 4. Three is the magic number — it allows anchoring (cheap/middle/expensive) without overwhelming.
  • Pre-select smart defaults. Default selected = decision avoided. But: pick defaults that benefit the user, not just you.
  • Use "Most popular" / "Recommended" badges to collapse decisions. They exploit social proof + guide attention.
  • Progressive disclosure for advanced options. 80% of users want the simple path; surface that. Hide complexity behind "Advanced" or "More options."
  • Onboarding: one decision per screen. Don't ask for name, role, team size, plan, and goals on one form. Split it.
  • Long lists: add search + categories.
